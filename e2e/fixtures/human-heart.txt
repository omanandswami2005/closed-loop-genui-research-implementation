The Human Heart and Circulation

The heart is a muscular organ about the size of a closed fist. It pumps blood around the body through a network of blood vessels. The human heart has four chambers: two upper chambers called atria and two lower chambers called ventricles. The right side of the heart receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs. The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the rest of the body. Because the left ventricle must push blood all the way around the body, its muscular wall is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle.

Humans have a double circulatory system. In the pulmonary circuit, blood travels from the heart to the lungs and back. In the systemic circuit, blood travels from the heart to the organs and tissues and back. Blood passes through the heart twice for every complete trip around the body. A double circulation keeps blood pressure high in the systemic circuit, so oxygen reaches the tissues quickly.

Valves inside the heart stop blood from flowing backwards. The tricuspid valve lies between the right atrium and right ventricle, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve lies between the left atrium and left ventricle. Semilunar valves sit at the exits to the pulmonary artery and the aorta. The "lub-dub" sound of a heartbeat is made by valves closing.

There are three main types of blood vessel. Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure; they have thick, elastic, muscular walls. The aorta is the largest artery. Veins carry blood back towards the heart under low pressure; they have thinner walls, a wider lumen, and valves to prevent backflow. Capillaries are tiny vessels with walls only one cell thick, which allows oxygen, glucose and carbon dioxide to diffuse between the blood and the body's cells.

The heart muscle itself needs a supply of oxygen, which it gets from the coronary arteries. If a coronary artery becomes narrowed by fatty deposits, the heart muscle can be starved of oxygen; this is coronary heart disease. Treatments include stents, which hold the artery open, and statins, which lower blood cholesterol.

The natural resting heart rate is controlled by a group of cells in the right atrium called the pacemaker (the sinoatrial node), which sends out electrical impulses that make the heart muscle contract. During exercise, heart rate and the volume of blood pumped per beat both increase, so more oxygen and glucose reach the working muscles and more carbon dioxide is removed. An artificial pacemaker is an electrical device that can be fitted to correct an irregular heartbeat.
