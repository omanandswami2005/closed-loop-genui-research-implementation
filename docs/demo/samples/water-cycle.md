# The Water Cycle

The water cycle (also called the hydrological cycle) describes how water moves continuously between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land. The total amount of water on Earth stays roughly the same; it changes state and location. The cycle is driven by energy from the Sun and by gravity.

## Evaporation and transpiration

Heat from the Sun turns liquid water from oceans, lakes and rivers into water vapour. This is **evaporation**. About 86% of global evaporation comes from the oceans. Plants also release water vapour through tiny pores (stomata) in their leaves; this is **transpiration**. Together they are often called *evapotranspiration*. Ice and snow can turn directly into vapour without melting first, which is called **sublimation**.

## Condensation and cloud formation

Warm, moist air rises and cools as it rises because air pressure is lower higher up. When the air cools to its **dew point**, the vapour condenses on tiny particles of dust, salt or smoke called condensation nuclei, forming droplets. Billions of these droplets make a cloud. Condensation releases latent heat, which warms the surrounding air and can fuel storms.

## Precipitation

When cloud droplets collide and grow heavy enough, they fall as **precipitation**: rain, snow, sleet or hail, depending on the temperature of the air they fall through. Precipitation returns water from the atmosphere to the surface.

## Collection: runoff, infiltration and groundwater

Water that reaches the ground can flow over the surface into streams and rivers as **surface runoff**, especially when the ground is saturated, frozen or paved. Some water soaks into the soil; this is **infiltration**. Water that percolates deeper fills the spaces in rocks, forming **groundwater**. Rock layers that store and transmit groundwater are called **aquifers**. Groundwater can flow slowly back into rivers and the sea, sometimes over thousands of years.

## Why the cycle matters

The water cycle moves heat around the planet, shapes landscapes through erosion, and supplies the fresh water that people, plants and animals need. Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and most of that is locked in ice caps and glaciers. Human activity changes the cycle: cutting forests reduces transpiration and increases runoff, and urban surfaces such as concrete reduce infiltration, which can increase the risk of flash floods. A warmer climate increases evaporation, so the atmosphere holds more water vapour and heavy rainfall events become more intense.
